New Digital Hall Pass System Frustrates Students and Staff
By Staff Writer
Wharton High School introduced a new digital hall pass system at the start of the semester, requiring students to request bathroom and hallway privileges through an app on school-issued devices rather than using the traditional paper slips teachers have relied on for years. The transition has not gone smoothly for everyone involved.
The system, called EduPass, is designed to give administrators real-time visibility into student movement across campus. In theory, a teacher approves a request on their device, a timer starts, and the student's location and duration are logged automatically. The goal, according to school communications sent at the beginning of the year, is to reduce hallway crowding during instructional time and create a cleaner record of student whereabouts.
In practice, students say the app is slow and sometimes doesn't load at all.
"Half the time I submit a request and it just spins," said sophomore Jordan Acevedo. "By the time it goes through I've already missed like ten minutes of class waiting to find out if I can leave."
Teachers have voiced similar frustrations, though many are more measured in how they describe it. Ms. Patricia Dunham, who teaches 10th grade English, said the system adds steps to what used to be a simple transaction.
"I understand why they want the data," she said. "It's just one more thing to manage when you're already managing thirty students."
The platform's parent company, EduPass Technologies, markets the software as a comprehensive safety and accountability tool used in over 2,000 schools nationwide. Whether Wharton's implementation reflects a configuration issue or a broader product limitation is not entirely clear from available information, and requests to the administration for comment were not returned before publication.

Student council has reportedly raised the hall pass issue in its last two meetings, though no formal resolution has been passed. Several teachers have informally reverted to paper backups on days when connectivity is unreliable, a workaround that somewhat defeats the purpose of the digital system.
Junior Tomas Brickell said the irony isn't lost on students.
"We're using paper anyway, so what was the point," he said.
The administration has not announced a review of the rollout or a timeline for addressing reported issues. EduPass is currently contracted through the end of the school year, meaning students and teachers are likely to keep navigating the system for the foreseeable future, bugs included.
Whether a smoother rollout could have been achieved with more pilot testing or teacher training beforehand is the kind of question this article probably should have pushed harder on. It didn't, but the frustration is real and the story, at least, is told.
