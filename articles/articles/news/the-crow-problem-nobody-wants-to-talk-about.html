The Crow Problem Nobody Wants to Talk About
By Staff Writer

Every morning at approximately 7:42 a.m., a small war breaks out in the east parking lot of Wharton High School.

The combatants: three to five American crows (Corvus brachyrhynchos), several abandoned Chick-fil-A containers, and the unwilling humans trying to navigate around them to get to first period. The crows, for their part, seem unbothered.

"I dropped my hash brown and one of them just — stared at me," said junior Marcus Delray. "Like it was waiting for me to apologize."

Crows have lived alongside humans for thousands of years, and researchers increasingly argue that the relationship is less parasitic than it is reciprocal. Studies out of the University of Washington have shown that crows can recognize individual human faces, remember perceived slights for years, and even bring small gifts — bottle caps, shiny wrappers, the occasional paperclip — to humans who feed them regularly.

Whether the Wharton parking lot crows have formed any such opinions about the student body remains, officially, unstudied.

What is documented: the birds are getting bolder. Assistant principal David Okafor confirmed that three separate complaints were filed last semester related to "crow interference," a category that did not exist in prior disciplinary logs. One incident involved a crow landing on a student's open backpack. Another involved what a witness described as "coordinated dive behavior" near the vending machines.

Biology teacher Ms. Carrie Hoffstead, who has taught AP Environmental Science at Wharton for eleven years, says the behavior is entirely normal — and, in her view, a little bit our fault.

"Crows are incredibly adaptable," she said. "We've built this large open campus with food waste everywhere and very few natural deterrents. From a crow's perspective, this is prime real estate."

Hoffstead estimates the campus population at somewhere between eight and fifteen individuals, possibly part of a larger extended group that roosts nearby. Crows are highly social animals and tend to establish feeding territories — meaning Wharton's parking lot flock is likely the same birds, every day, reliably mapping where the breakfast wrappers land.

Some students have leaned into it. Senior Priya Moonsamy has been leaving unsalted peanuts near the east entrance every Tuesday and Thursday for the past three months. She reports that two crows now approach her directly, while ignoring classmates walking alongside her.

"I think they know," she said simply, when asked for an explanation.

The administration has not announced any formal policy regarding the crows, and Florida's migratory bird protections would limit removal options regardless. For now, the birds remain — watching, waiting, cataloguing.

Marcus Delray has started holding onto his hash browns with both hands.
